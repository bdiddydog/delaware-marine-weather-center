How to Read Today's Marine Charts

LEGEND / EDUCATION

Surface Analysis

  • H / L: high / low sea-level pressure; nearby numbers usually in hPa (mb).
  • Orange curved lines: isobars, equal atmospheric pressure. On OPC charts, abbreviated labels such as 36 can signify 1036 mb according to the surrounding pattern.
  • Blue line + triangles: cold front; triangles point toward advance.
  • Red line + semicircles: warm front.
  • Purple front: occluded front; alternating red/blue symbols on opposite sides indicate stationary front.
  • GALE: warning for gale-force winds in the indicated offshore region—not automatically a warning for Delaware Bay.

Wind & Wave Chart

  • Numbers over water: significant wave height; check chart header for meters or feet.
  • Significant height: average of the highest third of waves, not the largest possible wave.
  • 1 m ≈ 3.28 ft: 2 m ≈ 6.6 ft; 5 m ≈ 16.4 ft; 7 m ≈ 23 ft.
  • Wave period: seconds between successive crests; not shown on every height chart.
  • Wave direction: check a separate swell-direction product; height by itself does not establish coastal impact.

500-mb Upper-Air Chart

  • Contours: geopotential heights, often labeled in decameters (e.g. 564 = 5,640 m).
  • Trough: equatorward dip in the height field; often associated with unsettled weather.
  • Ridge: poleward bulge; often associated with relatively stable patterns.
  • Wind barbs: short feather = 5 kt; full feather = 10 kt; pennant = 50 kt.
  • Important: 500-mb H/L features are not surface pressure centers. Compare with the surface chart.

🎓 Learn to Forecast — Marine Meteorology Tutor

INTERACTIVE TRAINING

Learn marine forecasting from the actual NOAA maps above. Begin with pressure → wind → waves, then compare your interpretation with the official NWS marine zone forecast. Training exercises are hypothetical and never represent current conditions.

1 • Pressure

Look at: surface highs and lows, frontal boundaries, and isobar spacing. Closely packed isobars imply a stronger horizontal pressure gradient, often supporting stronger wind.

2 • Wind

Look at: direction, sustained speed, gusts, and exposure. A NE wind blows from northeast toward southwest. A knot is roughly 1.15 mph.

3 • Waves

Look at: significant wave height, period, and swell direction. Height alone cannot tell you the direction of propagation or how waves will behave near an inlet.

4 • Local Geography & Hazards

Compare: sheltered Delaware Bay, Chesapeake Bay, and exposed Atlantic waters. Check official zone forecasts, NWS warnings, and available buoy observations before any boating decision.

Four-Week Learning Path

Week 1 — Winds & Pressure

Knots, wind barbs, isobars, pressure gradients, onshore/offshore wind.

Week 2 — Waves & Swell

Significant wave height, wave period, fetch, swell direction, Delaware Bay versus Atlantic exposure.

Week 3 — Tides & Hazards

Flood/ebb currents, wind against tide, coastal flooding, gale and small-craft warnings.

Week 4 — Forecast Integration

Surface and 500-mb charts, wave guidance, buoy verification, and concise marine discussions.
